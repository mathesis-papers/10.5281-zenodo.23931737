Proof of the weighted-minor inequality in MathOverflow 469781

Haobo Ma, Wenlin Zhang

Paper

Haobo Ma and Wenlin Zhang ask whether weighted coordinate areas generated by two vectors can be controlled by coordinate volumes obtained by adding a perpendicular direction. They answer yes: giving each volume the largest associated area weight provides an upper bound, while using the smallest provides a lower bound. Their argument allows negative weights and higher-dimensional analogues.

Take vectors x and y in real n-dimensional space, with n at least three, and a unit vector z perpendicular to both. The determinant pᵢⱼ = xᵢyⱼ − xⱼyᵢ records oriented area in coordinate directions i and j; the corresponding three-coordinate determinant qᵢⱼₖ records oriented volume for x, y and z. These determinants are called minors. Assign each unordered coordinate pair an arbitrary real weight. The authors bound the weighted sum of squared area minors between two sums of squared volume minors, using the smallest or largest pair weight within each triple. Neither unit length nor mutual perpendicularity is required of x and y.

The original question, posted by meler on Mathematics Stack Exchange and MathOverflow in April 2024, required an orthonormal triple and positive weights. In dimension three, the squared volume is one and the squared areas sum to one: the comparison simply bounds a weighted average. Higher dimensions involve overlapping triples. The authors credit Richard Lyons’s 2003 comparison of determinantal probability measures with already implying the original result, and Thomas Miller and Radosław Bistroń’s 2025 direct proof with establishing both bounds for complex orthonormal triples and nonnegative weights. This note offers another argument and broader hypotheses, not the first settlement.

The proof uses exterior algebra, which organizes alternating area and higher-dimensional volume data. It packages the area minors into one object and adjoins z to obtain the volume object. Because z is a perpendicular unit direction, the sum of squared coordinates is preserved. A reverse operation, called contraction, removes the z direction, recovers the area object, and never increases length. Crucially, recovering the area coordinate for a particular pair uses only volume coordinates whose triples contain that pair. This local dependence connects the algebra to the weighted comparison.

Choose any collection of coordinate pairs and retain every triple containing a chosen pair. Contraction followed by discarding unwanted coordinates shows that the collection’s total squared area cannot exceed those triples’ total squared volume. The authors then decompose nonnegative weights into finitely many threshold levels. At each level, a triple is retained exactly when its largest pair weight reaches that threshold. Adding the level contributions gives the weighted upper bound. For signed weights, shift all weights by a constant to make them nonnegative; equal total squared area and volume make the added contributions cancel. Applying the upper bound to negated weights gives the minimum bound.

The reasoning extends to complex vectors, using Hermitian perpendicularity and squared absolute values. It also covers alternating data of any degree, even data not expressible as an exterior product of individual vectors. Several mutually perpendicular unit directions can be adjoined successively when the original data lie in their common perpendicular space. Each larger coordinate set then uses the minimum or maximum weight over its original-size subsets. Constant weights give equality, and nonzero positive-constant examples show that the upper-bound coefficient one cannot be reduced.

A related motivation concerns distances between complex lines, represented by unit vectors with overall phase ignored. Starting with distances between coordinate labels, one weights squared area minors by those distances raised to p, sums, and takes a pth root. The manuscript cites Miller and Bistroń’s full proof that this defines a distance for every p ≥ 2, including the formerly conjectured p = 2 case. That general distance theorem is not proved anew here. The note reproduces the small-angle obstruction for p < 2 and proves the constant-coordinate-distance case for p ≥ 2.

The supporting evidence has different scopes. The authors identify a Lean declaration covering only the original real orthonormal-triple inequality with strictly positive symmetric weights. They also report finite exact rational checks in dimensions three through six and further exact complex-rational checks in dimensions four through six. These computations do not establish universal statements; the broader extensions rely on the written proofs, not the narrower Lean statement. The note’s significance is its short local-to-weighted mechanism and higher-degree generality. The linked distance conjecture is presented as already settled by earlier work, not as a remaining open problem.
